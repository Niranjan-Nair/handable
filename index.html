Typed text, written in your hand

You write the alphabet a few times on a drawing pad. The app learns how you shape each letter and how you space your words. After that, you type and it writes the text out for you, as an SVG or a PDF you can print.

  1. Write some letters

    Draw each character three to five times. If you have two ways of writing a letter, like a looped and a straight y, draw both. The app keeps them apart and remembers which one you reach for more often.

  2. Add the letters you join

    If your g runs into the next e, write ge a few times without lifting the pen. The app learns the join and how often you make it.

  3. Type and download

    Type anything, including math in TeX. Choose ink or pencil and plain or lined paper, then download it. Long text carries on to a second page.

Good to know

  • Your samples are saved in this browser and never uploaded. To back them up or move them to another computer, use Export in the library.
  • You don't need to draw every character. A capital Q you never wrote can borrow the curve of your O.
  • Each letter is a new mix of your samples, so a word never comes out exactly the same twice.
  • A stylus gives the most natural line. A mouse or trackpad works too, and the smoothness setting irons out the wobble.

Formatting and math help

<sup> <sub> <u> <s> and <box> tags work, and so do x² and H₂O. Highlight with <mark>, which is yellow, or pick a color: <mark pink> <mark orange> <mark green> <mark blue>. Straight quotes become curly ones, and an apostrophe is written like a closing quote.

Put math between $ signs (or \( and \), as the Equation button does), or between $$ (or \[ and \]) to give it its own centered line. A $ followed by a space or closing before a digit, as in prices like $5, stays a dollar sign. Write it in TeX: x^2 a_{ij} \frac{a}{b} \sqrt[3]{x} \alpha \le \times \to \overline{AB} \text{if}.

In $$ math, limits go underneath, as in \sum_{i=1}^n and \lim_{x\to0}. Elsewhere, add \limits, as in \int\limits_0^1. Integrals: \int \iint \iiint \oint \oiint \oiiint. \underset{below}{x} and \overset{above}{x} work on anything. Matrices work as in amsmath: \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}, with matrix bmatrix Bmatrix vmatrix Vmatrix smallmatrix and cases. & separates columns and \\ rows. \cdots \vdots \ddots fill in the gaps. \$ writes a dollar sign.

A long equation carries on to the next line after an =, + or other operator, the way TeX breaks it. Start a new line yourself with \\ or \newline, as in $$x = 1 \\ y = 2$$.

Fonts are written the way people write them by hand: \mathbb{R} doubles a stroke (ℝ ℕ ℤ work too), \mathcal{L} adds flourishes, and there are \mathfrak{g} \mathbf{v} \mathit{x} \textbf{} \textit{}. Boxes: \boxed{x = 4} and \fbox{text}. Strike out with \sout{}. Accents: \hat{x} \vec{v} \tilde{n} \dot{x} \ddot{x}.

Symbols include \forall \exists \in \notin \subseteq \cup \cap \emptyset \nabla \implies \iff \equiv \sim \cong \propto \perp \parallel \neg \land \lor \oplus \otimes \hbar \ell \aleph \langle \rangle \lfloor \rceil \therefore \not=.
